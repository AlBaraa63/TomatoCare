ABSTRACT

	TomatoCare is an offline, artificial-intelligence (AI) powered Android application for home gardeners and small-scale tomato growers in the United Arab Emirates. Non-expert growers struggle to identify tomato leaf diseases reliably, and the diagnostic apps currently available require a constant internet connection, offer no Arabic interface, and — being single classifiers — will confidently mislabel an out-of-scope photograph (another crop, a hand, an everyday object) as a tomato disease. Such confident-but-wrong advice leads to misapplied treatments, wasted pesticides, and crop loss. TomatoCare addresses this with a safety-first design that runs entirely on-device: it first verifies that an image is a tomato leaf before it will diagnose, presents calibrated confidence, and reports its real-world performance honestly.

	The system is a three-stage classification cascade: a leaf gate and a tomato gate (each a lightweight MobileNetV3-Small) reject non-leaf and non-tomato inputs before any diagnosis is attempted, and an eleven-class disease classifier (MobileNetV3-Large) then identifies one of ten tomato diseases or a healthy leaf. The classifier is trained on a PlantVillage-derived tomato dataset, and its confidence is calibrated by temperature scaling so that the 60% low-confidence threshold is statistically meaningful. On a held-out laboratory test set of 6,683 images the deployed cascade reaches 97.59% disease accuracy and 97.19% end-to-end accuracy; on real-world PlantDoc field photographs it reaches 77.2% end-to-end — a laboratory-to-field gap the project measures and reports openly rather than concealing. Each diagnosis is presented with the condition name in English and Arabic, a calibrated confidence score, a severity indicator (Low, Medium, High, or Critical), and treatment suggestions filtered by the user's growing method (greenhouse, open-field, hydroponic, or saline-soil); when confidence falls below 60% a Low Confidence Warning is shown instead of a result. All three models are exported to TensorFlow Lite with float16 quantisation, totalling 9.87 MB and running fully offline without compromising accuracy.

	TomatoCare is currently written in Kotlin along with Jetpack Compose, using CameraX to capture images and Kotlin Serialization to store scan history locally in a localized form, as a localized JSON. All the processing is done on-device without the need to be connected to the internet. The application supports both English and Arabic with full right-to-left layout support, and export and import scan history via the Android Storage Access Framework.

1	CHAPTER 1: INTRODUCTION
1.1	Introduction
One of the most widely grown vegetables in the UAE — cultivated by home gardeners, small-scale farmers, and commercial greenhouse operators alike — is the tomato (Solanum lycopersicum). Tomato production in the Gulf region faces challenges fundamentally different from those of temperate agriculture: summer temperatures exceeding 45 °C, saline groundwater, and intense solar radiation place tomato plants under severe physiological stress and weaken their natural defences against diseases caused by fungal pathogens, bacteria, and viruses [1].
For the non-expert grower, the central difficulty is identifying which disease a plant is suffering from. Tomato diseases such as early blight (Alternaria solani), late blight (Phytophthora infestans), septoria leaf spot, and bacterial spot present as overlapping patterns of leaf yellowing, wilting, and necrotic spotting that are difficult to tell apart without training. Misidentification leads to the wrong treatment being applied, wasted agricultural inputs, and — in severe cases — complete crop loss.
The diagnostic tools currently available fail to tackle this problem effectively. Commercial products such as Plantix and Agrio require a constant internet connection, which is not always available in rural or outdoor agricultural environments. None of these platforms offers an Arabic-language interface — a significant accessibility barrier, given that a large proportion of the UAE's agricultural workforce is Arabic-speaking. Professional agricultural consultants, meanwhile, remain out of reach of most small-scale growers for reasons of cost and geography.
TomatoCare is proposed to fill this gap. It is a native Android application that is offline, AI-powered and allows any grower, irrespective of their technical expertise, connectivity or economic status, to photograph a tomato leaf and receive an instant, precise diagnosis and treatment recommendations tailored to the UAE growing conditions. TomatoCare is a socially responsible venture in line with the UAE National Food Security Strategy 2051 [2], which seeks to democratise precision agriculture to those who have traditionally not enjoyed the benefits of precision agriculture.

1.2	Background and Motivation
1.2.1	The UAE National Food Security Strategy 2051 and the Digital Divide
The United Arab Emirates has made food security a national strategic priority, with the government acting as a long-term goal of achieving a significant improvement in the sustainability of local agriculture. The UAE National Food Security Strategy 2051 is the voice that manifests this commitment in three pillars of operations; developing local production capability, improving production efficiency, and using new technologies to enhance agricultural productivity [2]. The final vision will be to enable farmers with data-driven decision-making solutions that will reduce the reliance on imported food and enhance the agricultural resilience of the country.
Although such policy ambition, there is still a significant difference between the governmental intent and ground level practice. Big-scale commercial producers have the financial capability to finance precision agriculture technologies, but small-scale growers and home gardeners, who comprise a large and growing proportion of the UAE food-producing population, remain largely out of the advances. This imbalance leads to a largely reactive method of crop management: by the time a small-scale grower becomes aware of a disease problem by simply looking at the crop, the condition has typically escalated to a point where the grower is bound to lose a significant portion of his crop. TomatoCare responds to this knowledge gap by putting a specialised agronomic diagnostic tool directly in the hands of all growers with a smartphone, irrespective of their economic size and technical understanding.
1.2.2	The Accessibility Imperative: Offline Edge Computing
TomatoCare is based on an offline, edge computing platform. The processing of images and inference is all done on the device, and no network request is made anywhere during the process. This is not just a technical choice of architectural preference, but rather a need based on the realities of the proposed deployment environment. Mobile data coverage of rural farming areas in the UAE is quite unreliable or even not present at all, so cloud-dependent diagnostic tools cannot be reliably used in the field.
TomatoCare uses float16 quantisation to compress its three cascade models into TensorFlow Lite format for on-device inference, without compromising diagnostic accuracy. This keeps the combined memory footprint and run-time low enough for low-end Android devices with API level 26 (Android 8.0 Oreo) and higher and as little as 2 GB of RAM. The outcome is a diagnostic tool that is equally available to a grower working in a greenhouse in Abu Dhabi and one who is tending a home garden in a remote desert community, without any dependence on connection, subscription charges, or specialist hardware.
1.2.3	Growing Method Context
	The scope of growing practices in tomato cultivation in the UAE has a variety of growing techniques, each of which presents different disease dynamics and treatment needs. The advantages of greenhouse cultivation include being able to provide environmental control but the condition in which fungal pathogens such as Leaf Mold thrive. Open-field farming is exposed to the full blast of UAE sun rays and wind-carried pathogen. The root-zone bacterial infection is susceptible to hydroponic systems, which is increasingly becoming common in urban UAE settings. Saline-soil cultivation, practised where groundwater salinity is elevated, places additional physiological stress on the crop and is common in parts of the UAE. To rank the most relevant treatment recommendations to that particular cultivation environment, TomatoCare captures the growing approach used by the user at the point of scan and uses this contextual information to rank the most relevant treatment recommendations to that specific cultivation environment.
1.3	Proposed System
TomatoCare replaces the single-classifier design of the Capstone 1 prototype with a three-stage decision cascade. The application first verifies that the input image is a leaf, then that it is specifically a tomato leaf, and only then attempts a disease diagnosis — rejecting out-of-scope inputs (other crops, hands, everyday objects) before any prediction is shown to the user. All three models run on-device in TensorFlow Lite with no network access, preserving the offline-edge property described in §1.2.3.
After an image is captured (live via CameraX or selected from the device gallery), it is preprocessed once — resized to 224×224 and normalised — and the same preprocessed tensor is passed sequentially to each stage of the cascade. If the leaf gate or the tomato gate rejects the input, the user is shown a clear out-of-scope message rather than a confident wrong diagnosis. If both gates accept, the disease classifier produces a probability distribution over eleven conditions (ten diseases and a healthy class): Tomato Early Blight, Tomato Late Blight, Tomato Bacterial Spot, Tomato Septoria Leaf Spot, Tomato Spider Mites (Two-spotted Spider Mite), Tomato Target Spot, Tomato Yellow Leaf Curl Virus, Tomato Mosaic Virus, Tomato Leaf Mold, Powdery Mildew, and Healthy. The disease classifier's confidence is calibrated by temperature scaling so that the 60% low-confidence threshold reflects true reliability rather than raw softmax output, justified on ethical grounds in §4.6.
When the top confidence meets or exceeds the threshold, the application shows the primary diagnosis with the name in both English and Arabic, the calibrated confidence, a severity indicator (Low, Medium, High, or Critical), and localised treatment suggestions tailored to the user's growing method. Below the threshold, the application issues a Low Confidence Warning and prompts the user to retake the photograph in better conditions. Scan records are stored locally as JSON via kotlinx.serialization with no cloud dependency, and the user can export and re-import their history through the Android Storage Access Framework. The entire experience — cascade, treatment database, and all UI strings — runs offline, and the application additionally embeds an in-app feedback mechanism that labels and stores real field photographs for future retraining (§6.4).

Element	Description
The Problem Of	TomatoCare's recognition engine was developed in deliberate iterations, each responding to a measured limitation of the one before it. An initial from-scratch convolutional network (TomatoCareNet) established feasibility at 91.17% laboratory accuracy and confirmed the team could design and train a competitive architecture end-to-end; it was then upgraded — for the Capstone 1 prototype — to a single MobileNetV3-Large classifier extended with a not_tomato reject class, gaining transfer-learning accuracy and a smaller mobile footprint. That prototype, however, exposed a critical failure mode in real-world testing: non-tomato images — other crops' leaves, hands, everyday objects — were frequently classified as a tomato disease with high confidence. In an agricultural advisory context this is a safety defect, not merely an accuracy shortfall: a grower who photographs the wrong subject receives confident, wrong treatment advice.
The root cause was twofold. First, a single softmax head was forced to perform two conflicting jobs at once — out-of-distribution rejection and fine-grained disease discrimination — in one shared feature space, with the not_tomato class heavily under-represented. Second, and more subtly, all of the non-tomato training examples were clean laboratory images while the tomato examples included field photographs, so the model learned to separate images by photographic style (lab vs. field) rather than by leaf identity; a real field photo of another plant therefore "looked like" a tomato to the model.
The scope of the required solution was therefore not a better single classifier but a different decision structure: one that verifies an input is in-domain before attempting a diagnosis, that aligns training and inference preprocessing so style cannot leak in, and whose confidence outputs are trustworthy enough to drive a low-confidence warning. The remainder of this report describes how that decision structure — the three-stage cascade introduced in §1.3 — was designed, built, and evaluated. 1.6	Scope and Delimitations
1.6.1	Scope
	TomatoCare has its area covered along five dimensions. In terms of crop type, it is limited to tomato (Solanum lycopersicum) only. In terms of conditions, the disease classifier recognises eleven classes — ten diseases plus a healthy class — and the three-stage cascade (leaf gate → tomato gate → disease classifier) explicitly rejects inputs outside this scope before any diagnosis is produced. In terms of geographic context, the system is intended for deployment in the UAE and the Gulf region, where fully offline operation and Arabic-language support are essential for the target users. Concerning platform, the application targets native Android devices only (API 26+, ≥ 2 GB RAM), with all inference performed on-device through TensorFlow Lite. Full implementation details of the technology stack are documented in Chapters 3 and 6. In terms of language, the user interface is delivered in both English and Arabic with complete right-to-left layout support.
1.6.2	Delimitations
The following are specifically outside the scope of this project. Conditions outside the eleven trained classes are not diagnosed; a novel or untrained tomato condition produces a low-confidence warning rather than a confident wrong diagnosis. Multi-leaf and full-plant photographs are not supported — the cascade assumes a single, centred leaf — and multi-crop support is excluded as the model is trained on tomato leaf images only. Cloud synchronisation is not included; all data is stored on the user's device. Integration with Internet of Things sensors or environmental monitoring hardware is not supported, and the system accepts only smartphone camera input. iOS, user accounts, and authentication mechanisms are not implemented in this version. Finally, the application provides advisory treatment recommendations and is not a legally binding agrochemical prescription.
1.7	Significance of Study
	There are several contributions that TomatoCare makes that go beyond the scope of the immediate project. It first illustrates that it is possible to deliver precision agricultural diagnostics to low-end, offline devices without compromising the accuracy of the results, directly addressing the digital divide between large-scale commercial farming and smallholder agriculture in the UAE. Second, the three-stage cascade architecture demonstrates a generalisable pattern for safety-correct deployment of plant-disease classifiers: by hard-rejecting out-of-domain inputs before any diagnosis is produced, the system eliminates the confident-wrong-answer failure mode that has been documented but rarely addressed in the agricultural-AI literature. Third, TomatoCare reports an honest laboratory-to-field accuracy gap (97.19% lab vs 77.2% field on a matched subset) rather than a single benchmark figure, modelling a transparency standard that is conspicuously absent from commercial plant-diagnostic products. Fourth, TomatoCare fills a gap, which no currently available mobile application addresses, that leads to unnecessary application of pesticides and the environmental harm that it causes. Fifth, the project directly supports the UAE National Food Security Strategy 2051 by providing local growers with a tool that can minimize crop losses and enhance the sustainability of local food production. Lastly, the fact that the codebase and dataset contributions are open-source means that the work can be extended by the global research community, especially researchers working on arid-climate agricultural AI.

2.1	Introduction
	This chapter provides the academic and technical context of the design choices of TomatoCare and lays out the context of the system within the existing landscape of AI-based tools of diagnosing the plant. The recent developments in artificial intelligence, computer vision and mobile computing have revolutionized the process of plant disease detection, turning it into a more automated and image-based discipline [3, 4]. Despite this development, the literature indicates that there are still significant gaps in the literature when these tools are compared against the needs of small scale tomato production in is extremely dry climates such as the UAE.
	Sections 2.2 to 2.6 set out the technical and academic background for the major design choices in TomatoCare — convolutional neural networks for image-based plant-disease classification, the selection of a lightweight architecture suitable for mobile deployment, the choice of training data and the augmentation strategy, transfer learning, and the role of TensorFlow Lite quantisation in enabling on-device inference. Section 2.7 gives a comparative analysis of four existing applications (Farmonaut, Flora Incognita, Plantix, and Agrio). Section 2.8 discusses the privacy, ethical, and localisation considerations relevant to the UAE setting. Sections 2.9 to 2.11 cover out-of-distribution rejection and cascaded classifiers, confidence calibration, and the generative and segmentation techniques used in the domain-gap experiments. Section 2.12 synthesises the research gaps identified and the rationale for TomatoCare.
2.2	CNN-Based Detection of Plant Diseases: Basics.
Deep learning in plant disease classification application has evolved fast in the last decade. The early image-based diagnostic systems were based on conventional computer vision methods, like colour histogram analysis and feature extraction by hand, which performed reasonably well under controlled laboratory conditions but were much poorer at generalising to real-world field imagery. Convolutional neural networks provided a fundamental change in this paradigm as they made available the ability to learn hierarchical visual features directly and automatically based on labelled images [3].
Recent peer-reviewed investigations report that the state-of-the-art CNN designs can obtain classification accuracies of over 98 percent in the controlled plant disease datasets, with the corresponding F1 scores reaching the same level [3, 4]. Field deployments, where lighting, background and image quality differ significantly, usually report lower but still good accuracies in the range of 90% to 95% [3]. This is a well-documented phenomenon and has a direct implication for TomatoCare's evaluation methodology: rather than reporting only a laboratory benchmark, the project evaluates the deployed model on real-world field photographs as well, so that the laboratory-to-field performance gap is measured rather than assumed away (Chapter 7).
The CNNs are especially well adapted to the plant disease classification problem, since the diagnostic features that distinguish one pathology form another in the geometry of the necrotic lesions, the color transitions of the chlorotic tissue, the morphological patterns of the fungal mycelium, etc. are precisely the kind of localized visual patterns that CNN architectures are designed to detect through their convolutional and pooling layers [3, 4]. This fits between the architectural capability and the problem structure that informs the adoption of CNN-based approach to TomatoCare.
2.3	Lightweight CNN Architectures for Mobile Deployment
	Although CNNs have the diagnostic capacity that TomatoCare needs, the deployment environment low-end Android devices with a full-offline operating environment puts strict constraints on model size, memory footprint, and inference latency. Existing CNN architectures designed to run on a cloud-based inference, e.g., ResNet-50 and InceptionV3, cannot be used in this deployment case. An example is ResNet-50, which has about 25.6 million parameters and occupies around 98 MB on disk, which does not fit in the target APK size and the memory constraints of devices in the API 26 / 2 GB RAM category [5].
	MobileNet family of architectures was included to overcome this limitation. MobileNet models significantly reduce both the number of parameters and the cost of computation by an order of magnitude compared to traditional CNNs whilst maintaining most of their classification accuracy. The architecture used by TomatoCare, MobileNetV3-Large, is based on this architecture but with additions of Neural Architecture Search to optimise the layer structure to match the mobile hardware, the hard-swish activation function to improve gradient flow at a low computational cost, and squeeze-and-excitation blocks to enhance feature representation [6]. The resulting model has a size of about 5.4 million parameters; it is of size about 22 MB before quantisation and the best top 1 ImageNet accuracy of 75.2%.
	The decision of MobileNetV3-Large over MobileNetV2 (lower accuracy at similar size) and EfficientNet-B0 (slightly higher accuracy but slower inference on mobile hardware) can be explained by the fact that it was an intentional engineering decision to maximise diagnostic accuracy within the latency and storage constraint imposed by the offline, low-end-device deployment setting. Chapter 3, Section 3.6.3 makes a direct comparison of the relevant architectures.
2.4	PlantVillage Dataset and Domain Gap.
	PlantVillage dataset, initially gathered and published by Hughes and Salathé, has become a de facto standard benchmark in research on plant disease classification [9, 10]. The dataset comprises more than 54,000 labelled images across 38 classes and 14 crop species, including ten tomato classes (one healthy and nine diseased). TomatoCare's disease classifier is trained on a PlantVillage-derived tomato collection that extends this set to eleven classes by adding a powdery-mildew class (Chapter 3). The close match between the available data and the project's classification taxonomy motivated the choice of PlantVillage-derived data as the primary training source.
	Despite that, there is a well-documented limitation of PlantVillage that is commonly cited in the literature: all the images are taken in controlled laboratory conditions with uniform backgrounds and uniform lighting [9, 10]. When trained on images of plant life in the field, and with variable lighting, and cluttered backgrounds, or at non-standard camera angles, models trained only on PlantVillage typically have a sharp decrease in accuracy. This is also referred to as the domain gap, especially when models that have been trained on temperate-climate laboratory images are deployed to environments with very different visual properties such as the high-brightness, dust-affected, heat-saturated conditions characteristic of UAE outdoor and greenhouse agriculture.
	The domain gap is therefore the central evaluation challenge for any PlantVillage-trained system, and TomatoCare confronts it directly rather than assuming it away. The deployed disease classifier is trained with only minimal augmentation (horizontal flip); a heavier augmentation pipeline intended to simulate field conditions — brightness, contrast, gamma, colour, and blur jitter — was investigated as a way to close the gap but was found to *reduce* field accuracy and was therefore rejected. This is one of four controlled domain-gap experiments reported in Chapter 7. The measurement-driven approach reflects the literature's caution that augmentation strategies must be validated against real field data rather than assumed to help [10].
2.5	Transfer Learning and Fine-Tuning Strategies.
	The datasets needed to train a CNN when starting with random initialisation are large datasets containing millions of labelled images and large amounts of computational resources, both of which are impractical within the limits of a typical academic capstone project. Transfer learning offers an established alternative: a model that has been pre-trained on a large, general-purpose dataset (typically ImageNet, with over a million labelled images in a thousand categories) is adapted to a specialised task by replacing its classification head and fine-tuning a subset of its layers on the target dataset.
	It is confirmed in the literature that transfer learning provides considerable performance benefits in the classification of plant diseases in particular [9, 10]. ImageNet Pre-training the lower convolutional layers of a network with ImageNet pre-training gives the network general-purpose visual feature detectors edges, colour transitions, textures and basic shape primitives which are effectively transferred to the plant disease domain. The top levels, which combine more feature combinations specific to the task, are then re-trained on the target data to learn the visual representations of the conditions under classification.
	TomatoCare implements two-stage transfer learning strategy. During the first stage, all layers of the MobileNetV3-Large base model are frozen and only the new classification head is trained, which allows the classification head to adapt to the eleven tomato classes without disrupting the pre-trained feature representations. At the second stage, the top thirty layers of the original model are unfrozen and fine-tuned at a lower learning rate, allowing the model to adjust the higher-level feature combinations to the unique visual properties of tomato leaf conditions. These two phase methods which are further elaborated in Chapter 3 agrees with the current best practice in the transfer learning literature [6].
2.6	TensorFlow Lite and On-Device Inference.
	The fact that TomatoCare is designed to be operated entirely offline, without incurring any cloud inference, no network calls, no remote dependencies, etc. is a fundamental factor in the technical architecture of TomatoCare. There are four benefits of on-device inference that are directly applicable to the context of the UAE deployment: it does not rely on network availability, avoids latency of inference due to network roundtrips, ensures complete privacy of user data, and incurs no continuing operational cost.
	TensorFlow Lite is a framework by Google to deploy machine learning models on mobile and embedded devices. It takes as input a TensorFlow SavedModel and produces a small file (.tflite) in FlatBuffer format optimised to load quickly and run on mobile hardware. The framework supports a variety of quantisation strategies each with a different trade-off between model size, inference speed, and accuracy retention. Float32 (no quantisation) is the most accurate but yields the largest models. Int8 quantisation is the smallest and fastest but may introduce a significant amount of accuracy degradation, especially when fine-grained classification tasks are important. The Float16 quantisation, which TomatoCare adopted, is intermediate: it not only halts the model size but also preserves accuracy within a fraction of a percent of the unquantized baseline, making it the option of choice when the accuracy of the classification is of primary concern [11].
	When float16 quantisation is used to create the model, the model loads fast, fits in the available memory without a major issue, and runs within the latency budget that is available to support an interactive diagnostic experience. The joint operation of MobileNetV3-Large and float16 quantisation, therefore, constitutes a coordinated set of decisions: each of them is necessary, and they allow the offline, on-device operation that defines the architectural identity of TomatoCare.
2.7	Existing Plant Diagnostic Applications: Comparative Review
	Four exemplary AIs based plant diagnostic systems were chosen to undergo a thorough analysis: Farmonaut, Flora Incognita, Plantix, and Agrio. The four of them represent the full range of the current state of the art the large scale satellite-based commercial agriculture (Farmonaut), the citizen science species identification (Flora Incognita), and the smallholder-centered disease diagnosis (Plantix and Agrio) - and collectively can be seen as demonstrating both the capabilities and the shortcomings of the current state of the art.
2.7.1	Farmonaut 
  
Figure 3: Farmonaut application interface.
	Farmonaut is a precision agriculture platform that integrates satellite spectral tracking with smartphone-based image analysis by its Jeevn AI subsystem. The platform uses multispectral satellite data in the near-infrared and red-edge bands to detect large-scale crop anomalies, computing vegetation indices including NDVI, NDRE, EVI, SAVI, NDWI, and NDMI to measure the health of plants, their water status, and soil conditions across entire fields [15]. The satellite imagery is presented at a 10 metre resolution at 3 to 5 day intervals with synthetic aperture radar images provided in any case regardless of cloud cover.
	The Jeevn AI component uses CNNs to analyse images of crops captured by the smartphone to identify the presence of a disease based on the geometry of necrotic lesions, colour variation of the chlorotic colour, and the morphology of the fungal mycelium. Diagnostic accuracy is reported to be 98.32% in laboratory with F1 scores of 97.99 and about 90 percent to 95 percent in the field of deployment [16].
	Limitations and Applicability to TomatoCare. Although technically speaking, Farmonaut is simply out of place in terms of TomatoCare target users. The platform is a paid subscription platform and is designed to serve large commercial grade agricultural operations with satellite based monitoring requiring fields large enough to be meaningfully resolved at 10 metre resolution a scale not compatible with the home gardens and small plots typical of UAE smallholder cultivation [16]. Farmonaut does not provide an Arabic language interface, no offline capabilities, and no UAE-specific treatment recommendations [14]. Its model operation presupposes the extremely type of cloud connective and economical scale with which TomatoCare is planned to work without.
2.7.2	Flora Incognita
   
Figure 4: Flora Incognita application interface.
	A free scholarly plant identification software with research grant funding by the Max Planck Institute for Biogeochemistry and the Technical University of Ilmenau, is known as Flora Incognita [17]. The system employs hierarchical deep learning method whereby several CNNs sequentially process plant images, starting with low-level features (such as geometric form and texture) and progressing to high-level botanical structures (such as flowers and fruits). When the diagnostic confidence is small, the application requests more images of the specimen in order to refine its identification and the final classification is made on a dedicated server cluster.
	Over 30,000 vascular plant species have been identified in over 20 supported languages by Flora Incognita. A 2024 validation study conducted by a peer-reviewed study on the use of an independent reference dataset in ecological survey work reported a true positive rate of 98.8, establishing the platform as a scientifically credible tool in ecological survey work [18]. It is also capable of offline mode, game-based citizen science interactions via the Flora Capture programme, and fact sheets of information including plant toxicity data [19].
	Limitations and Applicability to TomatoCare. The main limitation of Flora Incognita as seen through the lens of TomatoCare is a category error: the application is only useful in identifying species, not diagnosing diseases. An accurate identification of the species (Solanum lycopersicum) would be obtained, but no data about the observed pathological condition would be provided [17, 18]. The training data is all images of wild, non cultivated ecosystems and contains no diseased crop samples. The site does not provide any treatment advice, no support in the Arabic language, and no agronomic advice that can be applied to UAE methods of cultivation.
2.7.3	Plantix Overview

	Plantix, made by PEAT GmbH, is one of the most actively implemented smartphone applications to identify plant diseases and pests all over the world. It uses CNN-based image recognition to identify diseases and pest damage on photographs uploaded by the user, supplemented by a peer-to-peer discussion forum that permits users to share their findings and seek community feedback. Plantix claims a reported identification accuracy of over 90 percent on diseases and pests across a wide variety of crop species in the field using an accumulated user-contributed image dataset to continuously optimize its models [16].
	Constrained and Relevant to TomatoCare. Although Plantix has a global presence, it has several features that constrain its use by tomato growers in the UAE. It is an inherently cloud-based application and requires a stable internet connection to operate - a limitation that rules out users in rural farming communities with poor connectivity [16]. The application does not have an Arabic language interface, which puts a significant barrier to accessibility of the program to the very large proportion of the agricultural workforce in the UAE that are Arabic speakers. Treatment suggestions are not localised to the UAE cultivation practice e.g. hydroponics or saline soils operation that minimises its practical applicability to the intended users of the project.

2.8	Privacy, Ethical and Localisation considerations.
	The usage of AI-based diagnostic tools in agricultural environments brings about a group of privacy, ethical, and localisation-related considerations, which are especially pertinent to the UAE environment. The offline-first architecture of TomatoCare offers a structural solution to many of these issues: since all image processing, scan storage, and diagnostic inference are performed solely on the device of the user, no user data, including images, scan history or device identifiers, is transmitted to any external server at any point during operation. This design complies with the principle of data minimisation of UAE Federal Decree-Law No. 45 of 2021 on the Protection of Personal Data [13], which requires that the information collected and processed should be strictly necessary to perform the diagnostic function, and it eliminates the cross-border data transfer issues that affect competitors in cloud-based solutions.
	Ethically, the application will be crafted to deliver diagnostic suggestions, as opposed to the clinical judgments. The 60 percent confidence level, below which the system will issue a Low Confidence Warning instead of a confident diagnosis, is an artificial ethical constraint that is meant to ensure that the system does not encourage users to act on an uncertain result. All the treatment recommendations are introduced as guidance to support rather than to replace professional agronomic advice, and the implementation has a clear disclaimer to this effect on its results screen.
	In this regard, localisation goes beyond translation. Bilingual support needs to be effective, meaning that in addition to the provision of Arabic-language strings to all interface text and treatment recommendations, the full right-to-left layout adaptation, mirrored navigation elements and culturally appropriate usage of agricultural and botanical terminology should be provided. TomatoCare meets these needs by building on the native RTL support of Jetpack Compose, by providing full dual-language resources of strings and by sourcing Arabic terms to established agricultural sources. The resulting implementation, ensuring that the application is both functionally and culturally accessible to the significant Arabic-speaking proportion of the UAE agricultural workforce, instead of treating Arabic support as an overlay of translation onto an underlying product based on the English language.
2.9	Out-of-Distribution Rejection and Cascaded Classifiers
A classifier deployed to non-expert users must handle inputs it was not trained on. Hendrycks and Gimpel established softmax-confidence baselines for detecting misclassified and out-of-distribution inputs and showed that a plain softmax is an unreliable rejector — high confidence does not imply in-distribution. A single classifier with an added reject class inherits this weakness: the reject decision competes with the discrimination task in one shared representation. Decomposing the problem into a cascade of focused stages, each with a dedicated in/out-of-domain objective, is a well-established alternative that gives each decision its own representation and lets the system stop early. TomatoCare adopts this cascade structure specifically to fix the Capstone 1 prototype failure in which a shared reject head misclassified non-tomato inputs with high confidence.
2.10	Confidence Calibration
Modern deep networks are typically overconfident: their reported probabilities do not match their empirical accuracy. Guo et al. characterised this and showed that temperature scaling — dividing the logits by a single learned scalar T before the softmax — is a simple, effective post-hoc calibration that minimises negative log-likelihood on a held-out set while leaving the argmax (and thus accuracy) unchanged. Calibration quality is measured by the Expected Calibration Error (ECE), the weighted average gap between confidence and accuracy across probability bins. TomatoCare applies temperature scaling to its disease classifier because the application's low-confidence warning is only meaningful if the underlying probabilities are calibrated.
2.11	Generative and Segmentation Techniques
Two further techniques were used in the project's domain-gap experiments. Deep Convolutional GANs (DCGAN) generate images by adversarial training of a convolutional generator against a discriminator; they are a standard method for synthetic data augmentation and were investigated as a way to enlarge the weakest disease class. The Segment Anything Model (SAM) is a promptable zero-shot segmentation model; MobileSAM is a lightweight distillation suitable for commodity hardware. Both were applied to test whether isolating the leaf from its background could close the laboratory-to-field gap. As Chapter 7 reports, neither improved field accuracy — a result that is consistent with the domain-gap literature: synthetic images reproduce the distribution they were trained on, and background removal cannot change the leaf's own field appearance.
2.12	 Research Gaps and Conclusion
The literature review outlines four research and product gaps that intersect to establish the design rationale behind TomatoCare:
1.	Cloud-dependent architectures that do not support low-connectivity users. Three of the four reviewed apps (Farmonaut, Plantix, Agrio) require a constant internet connection, making them essentially unusable in the rural and outdoor environments of small-scale UAE agriculture [14, 16].
2.	Lack of Arabic-language support. None of the reviewed applications offers an Arabic interface, even though Arabic speakers represent a significant proportion of the UAE agricultural workforce.
3.	Lack of UAE-localised treatment recommendations. The reviewed platforms give recommendations geared to temperate-climate practice and do not address UAE-specific cultivation methods such as hydroponic systems, saline-soil farming, or extreme-heat management [26].
4.	Unreported laboratory-to-field gap. Models trained on laboratory datasets such as PlantVillage are well known to lose accuracy on real field imagery, yet none of the reviewed platforms reports its field performance separately from its laboratory benchmark, leaving the accuracy a grower can actually expect unmeasured.
	TomatoCare is structured to address all four gaps. The application combines a PlantVillage-derived training pipeline with a safety-correct three-stage cascade, an offline-first architecture quantised to float16 for on-device inference, full bilingual English/Arabic support with right-to-left layout adaptation, and a treatment knowledge base localised to the four cultivation methods (greenhouse, open-field, hydroponic, saline-soil) common in the UAE. Crucially, it reports its real-world field accuracy openly rather than only a laboratory benchmark. These design choices rest on the literature reviewed in Sections 2.2 through 2.11: convolutional neural networks for image classification, lightweight architectures for mobile deployment, transfer learning, TensorFlow Lite quantisation, out-of-distribution rejection via cascaded classifiers, and confidence calibration.
	This chapter has shown that the combination of features offered by TomatoCare is unparalleled among the current platforms, that each technical decision has its basis in the current state of the art, and that the gaps that the application is designed to address are real, documented, and consequential. 3.3	Software Development Lifecycle
	The Software Development Lifecycle (SDLC) is a formal model of the activities involved in producing a software system, starting with the requirements gathering process and culminating with final deployment and validation [24]. SDLC models vary in how they order these activities, some following a linear, phase-by-phase sequence, and others utilizing iterative or incremental cycles, but the selection of model has major practical implications on projects where there is uncertainty, experimentation or changing requirements.
	TomatoCare uses an SDLC that is based on Agile. In contrast to linear models, like the Waterfall approach, where each phase must be finished before the next one begins, Agile breaks down the project into a sequence of short time-boxed iterations called sprints. In a sprint, a working increment of the system is produced, which is then assessed and utilized to inform planning of the next sprint. This iterative design enables the project team to adapt to findings - such as realizing that a certain augmentation plan is yielding overfitting, or that a particular user interface element is unintuitive on an Arabic RTL layout by adjusting the plan without necessarily re-initiating the entire process.
	There are three reasons why Agile approach fits best at TomatoCare. First, the development of machine learning models is inherently experimental: the accuracy of the model depends on a complex interaction of the quality of the dataset, the methods of its augmentation, the architecture determined, and hyperparameter settings, and the most effective combination is impossible to know in advance. Repeat training and assessment of the model are essential to achieve a model that fulfils the 90 percent accuracy criterion. Second, there needs to be a strong coordination of the machine learning and mobile development streams with the Android application, which must be managed by frequent synchronisation and incremental integration as opposed to a single big-bang integration at the end. Third, the offline-first architecture introduces constraints, such as APK size, memory footprint, inference latency, which must only be validated by testing on actual target devices, and which Agile manages to remind developers of by having them write software at the end of each sprint.

3.8	AI Model Development Methodology
The cascade design, training procedure, calibration, and on-device export described in this section together specify how the AI subsystem is built and what evidence is gathered to demonstrate that the design objectives in §1.5 are met. The Agile process and team workflow under which this work was carried out are described in §§3.1–3.7.
3.8.1	Datasets
The cascade is trained from three labelled sources, each serving a distinct stage. The disease classifier (Stage 3) is trained on a tomato leaf-disease collection referred to here as tomato20k; the gates (Stages 1 and 2) are trained on smaller balanced datasets specifically constructed to separate leaf from non-leaf and tomato from non-tomato. All sources are kept separate so that gate decisions cannot leak from the disease classes.
Stage 3 — disease classifier (tomato20k, 11 classes). tomato20k is a PlantVillage-derived tomato collection augmented with a powdery mildew class not present in the original PlantVillage tomato subset. The eleven classes are: bacterial_spot, early_blight, late_blight, leaf_mold, powdery_mildew, septoria_leaf_spot, spider_mites, target_spot, tomato_mosaic_virus, tomato_yellow_leaf_curl_virus, and healthy. The combined collection contains 25,851 training images and 6,683 held-out test images. The training split is further divided 85/15 into train and validation (seed 42, stratified per class); the held-out test set is the basis for every Stage 3 figure reported in Chapter 7.
Stage 2 — tomato gate. Positives are the tomato leaves above; negatives are non-tomato crop leaves — 4,627 PlantVillage pepper and potato images plus PlantDoc non-tomato field leaves. Mixing real-field PlantDoc images into the negatives is deliberate: without them, a model trained only on laboratory positives and laboratory negatives would learn to separate images by photographic style (lab vs field) instead of by leaf identity, exactly the failure mode that motivated the cascade in the first place (§1.2).
Stage 1 — leaf gate. Positives are leaf images (tomato and other crops); negatives are natural-world non-leaf images (people, animals, vehicles, everyday objects) drawn from an ImageNette-style natural-image source. The gate is small (MobileNetV3-Small) because the binary in/out-of-domain decision needs little capacity.
PlantDoc field data. 824 tomato field images are folded into the Stage 2/3 training splits, and 79 tomato field images are held out as the field benchmark used in Chapter 7. PlantDoc has no target_spot or powdery_mildew images, so the field benchmark is a 9-class subset of the 11 deployed classes.
3.8.2	Preprocessing and Parity
Every image — in both the Python training pipeline and the Android Kotlin inference engine — passes through an identical, contract-fixed pipeline: decode to RGB (3-channel); center-crop to the largest centred square (no squash-to-square distortion); resize to 224×224 using bilinear interpolation; scale to [0,1] by dividing by 255; and no ImageNet mean/std normalisation — the network is built to consume [0,1] directly. The input tensor is therefore float32[1, 224, 224, 3].
Center-crop (rather than naive resize-to-square) preserves leaf morphology — the lesion size and shape ratios that distinguish diseases — and corrects a v1 prototype defect in which the Python and Kotlin sides used different aspect-ratio handling. Preprocessing parity is part of the model-asset contract (§5.5) and is regression-tested: the Kotlin engine and the Python pipeline must produce numerically identical tensors for the same input image, otherwise the on-device probabilities cannot be trusted to match the Python evaluation figures.
3.8.3	Cascade Architecture
The system is a sequential cascade of three independent classifiers. A rejection at either gate halts the pipeline and returns a retake instruction to the user; only inputs that pass both gates reach the disease classifier. The gates use the MobileNetV3-Small backbone because a binary in/out-of-domain decision needs little capacity, while the disease classifier uses MobileNetV3-Large for the harder eleven-class problem.
This is the central architectural decision of the project. A single classifier must solve out-of-distribution rejection and fine-grained disease discrimination with one softmax head — conflicting objectives that share one representation, with the rare reject class fighting for capacity against ten common disease classes. The Capstone 1 prototype exhibited the predicted failure: non-tomato inputs were classified as a tomato disease with high confidence. Splitting the decision into three stages gives each its own representation and lets the easy decisions (leaf? tomato?) be made small, fast, and confidently before the hard one is even attempted.

The cascade is the third iteration of the project's recognition engine, and each prior iteration directly shaped it. The first version, TomatoCareNet, was a custom convolutional network built from scratch — four convolutional blocks with squeeze-and-excitation attention and global average pooling — trained across four merged datasets (PlantVillage, PlantDoc, TomatoVillage, and a supplementary Kaggle tomato set). It reached 91.17% on a held-out test split and established two things: that the team could implement and train a competitive architecture end-to-end, and that high laboratory accuracy alone did not guarantee reliable behaviour on out-of-scope inputs. The second iteration replaced the bespoke network with transfer learning on a single MobileNetV3-Large backbone plus a not_tomato reject class, which raised in-domain accuracy and compressed the model for mobile deployment, but surfaced the safety defect analysed above: one softmax head cannot reliably reject out-of-domain inputs and discriminate diseases at the same time. The third and deployed iteration — the three-stage cascade — resolves that defect by giving the in-domain decision its own dedicated gate models ahead of the disease classifier. Each step was a deliberate engineering response to a limitation measured in the previous one, and the progression from a hand-built CNN to a calibrated, safety-correct cascade is itself a result of the project.
3.8.4	Training
All three stages follow the same two-phase transfer-learning recipe. In Phase 1, head training (frozen backbone), the ImageNet-pretrained backbone is frozen and only the new head is trained: optimiser Adam with learning rate 1e-3, loss categorical cross-entropy with label smoothing 0.05. In Phase 2, fine-tuning, the top ~30 backbone layers are unfrozen and trained at a lower learning rate (Adam 1e-4) to adapt high-level features to leaf pathology without destroying low-level ImageNet features.
Class imbalance (e.g. powdery_mildew is the smallest class) is handled with inverse-frequency class weights. Training uses EarlyStopping (patience 5 on validation accuracy, best weights restored) and ReduceLROnPlateau (factor 0.5, patience 3 on validation loss). The deployed Stage 3 variant is the minimal-augmentation model (“ctrl”, horizontal flip only) — the augmentation comparison and its findings are reported in Chapter 7.
3.8.5	Confidence Calibration
Deep classifiers are typically overconfident, which would make the application’s 60% low-confidence banner meaningless. Temperature scaling [27] is applied to Stage 3: a single scalar temperature T is fit by minimising negative log-likelihood on a held-out set, then logits are divided by T at inference. Temperature scaling does not change the argmax (and so does not change accuracy), but it does change the calibration of the reported probability. The fitted temperature is T = 0.5889; the resulting Expected Calibration Error (ECE) figures are reported transparently in Chapter 7 — 0.0046 in-sample and 0.061 on the honest held-out test — and the gap motivates the dedicated calibration-set work flagged in Chapter 9.
3.8.6	Export and On-Device Deployment
Each stage is exported to TensorFlow Lite with float16 weight quantisation (≈ 2× smaller than float32; inputs and outputs remain float32 so the Android layer is unchanged). The three deployed artifacts are stage1_leaf_float16.tflite (MobileNetV3-Small, 1.92 MB), stage2_tomato_float16.tflite (MobileNetV3-Small, 1.92 MB), and stage3_disease_float16.tflite (MobileNetV3-Large, 6.03 MB), for a combined 9.87 MB — well within the 15 MB model budget defined in NFR-04 (§4).
On Android, the three interpreters run in sequence; a gate rejection short-circuits the pipeline so no unnecessary inference is performed. Class order and the preprocessing parameters are baked into the model-asset contract (§5.5), so updating any single model requires only swapping the .tflite file and the label list — no Kotlin code change.

























3.9	Data Persistence: JSON File Storage
	TomatoCare stores scan history and diagnosis result and user settings in a JSON file in the application internal storage directory (context).filesDir) [20]. The data classes representing scans, results and treatments are serialised and deserialised using Kotlin Serialisation [21].
	A storage in JSON files is chosen instead of a relational database, like Room or SQLite, due to six tangible reasons. First, the data model is conceptually simple an unstructured sequential list of scan records nothing more and does not need the relational joins, aggregate operations, or complex queries that justify the operational overhead of a relational database. Second, the project does not incur the schema migration expenses that would be incurred by changing data structures with time; updating the data model only requires reworking a Kotlin data class, as opposed to writing database migration scripts. Third, JSON fits perfectly with the export and import capabilities of the current application, as the storage format on the device and the export format are identical: since the on-device format and the format to be exported are the same, no separate serialisation layer is needed to back up and restore information. Fourth, the atomic write pattern (write to a temporary file, then rename to the target file) is crash safe because there is no possibility of leaving the JSON file in a corrupted state in the event of the application being interrupted during a write. Fifth, the volume of data is modest -a typical user produces a small number of scans per day, each occupying about 500 bytes, so even a thousand scans only occupies a little under one megabyte, well within the range of performance of file-based I/O. Sixth, Android internal storage security guarantees of the sandboxed per-application access and on-device encryption on Android 10 and above are automatically applied to the JSON file without any further cryptographic effort on the part of the application.
	The structure of a JSON file is that of an array of scan records, each containing an integer scan identifier, the path to the image, an ISO 8601 timestamp, the growing method selected by the user, the version of the model used when the scan was made, and an array of diagnosis results. Each result includes the most probable condition name, a primary-result flag, a confidence measure, a severity level, a static stress_type label (descriptive metadata, not a separate prediction), and an array of treatment objects containing treatment type, urgency level, and bilingual recommendation strings. Chapter 5 provides and discusses the entire schema.

	The deep learning framework based on TensorFlow is applied to the construction, training, and evaluation of the CNN model. All model development and dataset preparation work is in Python. PlantVillage dataset is obtained as publicly available repositories on Kaggle. The native Android app is created using Kotlin and Jetpack Compose [23], which combined give a modern declarative UI toolkit with an inbuilt support of a right-to-left layout necessary to localise the app to Arabic. 5	CHAPTER 5: DESIGN
5.1	System Architecture
	It was decided that a layered architecture will be used for TomatoCare. It will be a three-layer offline-first architecture that divides the system into 3 parts: a Presentation Layer, an Application Logic Layer, and a Data Layer. This architecture was chosen because:
1-	It provides modularity. where each component can be separately tested and replaced without affecting the rest of the layers. This is important in TomatoCare because testing or replacing the model, for example, would become nearly impossible to do independently if the design was monolithic.
2-	It’s offline-first because it enables farmers who work in remote, rural areas to use the application. Internet connection in these areas is not guaranteed, which makes architectures such as cloud-based designs useless.
5.1.1	Layer 1 – Presentation Layer (UI)
	For the presentation layer, Kotlin was used for implementation through Jetpack Compose. This layer creates what the user sees, from the home screen, camera capture screen, diagnosis results screen, etc. Jetpack Compose over XML-based layouts because of the declarative nature that reduces boilerplate, its built-in LayoutDirection.RTL support handles Arabic right-to-left text without needing a parallel XML hierarchy, and it is the current recommended Android UI toolkit. CameraX is part of this layer and provides a unified camera API that abstracts away different camera differences between devices, and results in a consistent image capture across all of them.
5.1.2	Layer 2 – Application Logic Layer (Inference Engine)
	The Application Logic Layer can be divided into 2 core parts: the image preprocessing pipeline and the TensorFlow Lite inference engine. The preprocessing pipeline takes the image from the presentation layer and resizes it to 224 × 224 pixels and normalises pixel values to the [0, 1] floating-point range as required by the model. The result is a tensor that is passed to the TensorFlow Lite inference engine. which loads the quantized model and performs the on-device inference.
	The engine then returns a ranked list of class probabilities across the eleven output classes. If the confidence rate of the top prediction is greater than or equal to 60%, it is passed to the Data Layer for storage, and to the Presentation Layer for display. Else, the engine returns a low-confidence flag that causes the UI to ask the user to either proceed or retake the photo. This is done to balance between  precision against usability for the users.
5.1.3	Layer 3 – Data Layer (Local Persistence)
	The Data Layer, which is responsible for reading and writing operations, takes the scan record and serialize it to a JSON file format using Kotlin’s ‘kotlinx.serialization’ library.  	First, localisation: the UI string resources and the bundled treatment database is stored in parallel English and Arabic versions. 5.8	Architectural Placement of the AI Subsystem
The application follows a layered architecture in which the AI cascade occupies a dedicated inference layer between the presentation layer (camera and gallery capture, results UI) and local storage. The inference layer is self-contained: it receives a bitmap, applies the preprocessing contract specified in §3.8.2, runs the three-interpreter cascade in sequence, and returns either a rejection reason (not a leaf / not a tomato) or a diagnosis with its calibrated confidence and severity indicator. This layer has no knowledge of the UI, no knowledge of persistence, and no network capability — properties that fall out of the design and are not bolted on.
5.9	Cascade Inference Design
The inference layer loads three TensorFlow Lite interpreters once at application start-up and runs them in sequence, short-circuiting on the first gate rejection. The leaf gate runs first; on rejection the pipeline returns NOT_A_LEAF and no further inference is performed. If the input passes the leaf gate, the tomato gate runs; on rejection the pipeline returns NOT_A_TOMATO. Only if both gates accept does the disease classifier run and return the eleven-class probability vector. Each stage applies the same preprocessing, invokes its interpreter, and reads the softmax output. Because the stages are independent interpreters, a gate rejection costs only the two small (1.92 MB) gate models and never invokes the larger 6.03 MB disease model — the cascade is cheaper on rejected inputs than on accepted ones, which is the correct cost profile for an out-of-domain rejection path.
5.10	Preprocessing-Parity Contract
The design pins the preprocessing contract (§3.8.2) in a labels.json file and mirrors it in the Kotlin ImagePreprocessor. The contract — center-crop to square, resize to 224×224 by bilinear interpolation, divide by 255, RGB channel order, no ImageNet mean/std normalisation — is the single most safety-critical interface in the system, because any silent divergence between training and inference preprocessing degrades accuracy without throwing an error. An automated parity check guards the contract at export time: the same fixture image is run through the Python pipeline and the Kotlin engine, and the resulting tensors must match to within floating-point tolerance before the build is published.
5.11	Model-Asset and Label Contract
The three *_float16.tflite files plus a labels.json file (which records each stage's class order and pass_class) and a treatments.json file (per-condition advice keyed by the Stage 3 class names) together constitute the deployed model contract. Class names are snake_case strings shared verbatim between the Python pipeline and the Android assets, so there is one canonical label set across training, evaluation, and the application — no name conversions in the inference path, and no possibility of two-system label drift. Updating any single model in production requires only swapping the corresponding .tflite file and — if the class set changed — the labels.json entries; no Kotlin code change is required.
6	CHAPTER 6: IMPLEMENTATION
6.1	Introduction
This chapter documents the implementation of the AI model pipeline and its on-device integration. The full application implementation — screen-level code, persistence layer, navigation — is documented in the application teammate's chapters and only referenced here where it touches the model.
6.2	Model Pipeline
The training and deployment pipeline is a sequence of Python scripts in the ml/ tree of the project repository. Each script has a single responsibility and writes its output to a fixed location, so the pipeline can be re-run end-to-end from raw datasets to deployable .tflite files in one command.
Dataset construction (build_dataset.py, integrate_plantdoc.py, prepare_*) assembles the tomato20k splits, the gate datasets, and folds in the PlantDoc field images.
Training (train.py) implements the two-phase transfer-learning recipe described in §3.8.4 with selectable augmentation modes (minimal = flip only, the deployed configuration; lighting; heavy; none) and inverse-frequency class weights.
Calibration (calibrate.py) fits the temperature scalar T on validation logits and bakes it into the final Dense layer (W ← W / T, b ← b / T), leaving the argmax — and therefore accuracy — unchanged while making the reported probabilities meaningful.
Export (export.py) converts each Keras model to float16 TFLite and runs the preprocessing-parity check that gates deployment. A build that fails parity is rejected before the assets reach the Android project.
Evaluation (eval_deployed_tflite.py) runs the shipped TFLite cascade against the held-out test set and writes a single eval_deployed.json file — the canonical source of truth for every quantitative result reported in Chapter 7. Every figure in Chapter 7 that names a measured number can be traced back to this file.
6.3	On-Device Cascade Integration
The Android inference layer (TFLiteEngine.kt) loads the three TensorFlow Lite interpreters from the application's assets bundle at start-up and implements the cascade specified in §5.9. The ImagePreprocessor implements the five-step preprocessing contract — decode to RGB, center-crop to the largest centred square, resize to 224×224, divide by 255, write to an NHWC float32 ByteBuffer — identically to the Python pipeline. The center-crop step was the critical correction from the Capstone 1 prototype, in which non-square photos were squashed to square and the leaf morphology required for fine-grained disease discrimination was distorted before inference.
No INTERNET permission is declared in the Android manifest. The bundled assets are the three .tflite files, labels.json, and treatments.json; no other model-related artefacts are pulled at runtime. A build inspection (§7.8) verifies both the absence of the permission and the bundled-asset list.
6.4	In-App Feedback Flywheel
To close the laboratory-to-field gap with real data over time, the application implements a lightweight feedback mechanism. After each diagnosis the results screen offers a one-tap “Was this correct?” confirm/correct control; the outcome is stored on the local ScanRecord; and a background exporter (TrainingDataExporter.kt) packages the labelled photographs into a ZIP organised by correctedConditionId/ subfolder — the exact directory layout that the training pipeline's image_dataset_from_directory expects. The ZIP can be copied off the device via the Android Storage Access Framework and fed back into a future training run without any reformatting or relabelling step.

7.1	Evaluation Framework
The cascade is evaluated on four axes, each targeting a distinct failure mode:
1.	Stage-3 disease accuracy on the held-out laboratory test set — disease-discrimination quality.
2.	Gate behaviour — how many genuine tomato leaves pass both gates (false-reject rate), and how many out-of-scope inputs are rejected (rejection recall / leak rate).
3.	End-to-end accuracy — the fraction of tomato test images that pass both gates and receive the correct diagnosis. This is the number the user experiences.
4.	Field accuracy — end-to-end accuracy on real-world phone photographs (PlantDoc).

7.3	Confidence Calibration
Temperature scaling (Guo et al., 2017) was applied to Stage 3, with the scalar temperature baked into the final dense layer (T = 0.5889) so that argmax — and therefore accuracy — is unchanged. On the temperature-fitting validation split, in-sample ECE fell from ≈0.07 to 0.0046. Measured on the held-out test set, the deployed ECE is 0.061 — a 4× degradation, consistent with the validation split also being seen during early stopping. Both numbers are reported here for honesty: the in-sample figure shows the temperature-scaling step worked as designed; the held-out figure is what the user actually experiences.
We therefore report 0.061 as the deployment figure: confidence is reasonably, though not tightly, calibrated. The practical implication is that the 0.60 low-confidence banner fires rarely on clean laboratory images (where confidences are high) and more usefully on lower-quality field images. A dedicated, unseen calibration set is required to substantiate a tighter figure (§9).
7.4	Field Validation and the Laboratory-to-Field Gap
The deployed cascade was evaluated on PlantDoc tomato field photographs (n = 79 held-out field images — real cluttered backgrounds, natural light, phone-camera quality). End-to-end accuracy is 77.2% and field disease accuracy is 87.1%, against 97.19% / 97.59% on the laboratory test set — a ≈20-point gap. The gates remain in scope: 96.2% of field tomato leaves pass both gates; non-tomato leaves continue to be rejected at high rates. The drop is therefore concentrated in the disease classifier on field-style leaf appearance.
Methodological caveat (disclosed): PlantDoc's own test partition was folded into the early-stopping validation split, so absolute field numbers are mildly optimistic and n is small; but the caveat applies equally to all model variants compared in §7.5, so the relative deltas are fair.

•	Background-independent (strong): late_blight (15/15), mosaic_virus (15/15), yellow_leaf_curl_virus (14/15) — distinctive shape/colour signals.

7.6	Synthesis
The experiments share one root cause and one conclusion. Interventions that operate within the laboratory distribution (augmenting it, cleaning its backgrounds, or synthesising more of it) cannot manufacture the field distribution, and transforming a field image at inference cannot reconstruct a laboratory image. The remaining gap is real-world leaf appearance under natural light, on phone-camera sensors, against cluttered backgrounds — a distribution shift that only labelled real field data can close. The in-app feedback flywheel (§6.4) is the deliberate response to this finding: rather than overclaim through synthetic augmentation, the system collects the actual operating distribution from real users.
7.7	Capability Statement
The system can: diagnose the eleven trained conditions on clean, single-leaf images at 97.59% accuracy; reject non-leaf inputs (99.55%) and non-tomato leaves before diagnosis (0.05% unseen-species leak); present calibrated confidence and warn when uncertain; run fully offline in 9.87 MB.
The system cannot (known edge cases): match laboratory accuracy on real field photographs (77.2% end-to-end), and is weakest in the field on the dark-lesion, background-dependent classes — early_blight, bacterial_spot, target_spot; reliably separate the visually similar early_blight / septoria / late_blight / leaf_mold cluster on hard examples (recalls 0.94–0.98); diagnose conditions outside the eleven trained classes (a low-confidence warning is surfaced instead).

8	CHAPTER 8: CONCLUSION
	Tomato production in the United Arab Emirates faces challenges that differ markedly from those of temperate agriculture, and the growers most exposed to them — home gardeners and smallholders — are also those least served by existing diagnostic tools. For a non-expert, identifying which disease a tomato plant is suffering from is genuinely difficult: many tomato diseases present as overlapping patterns of leaf yellowing, spotting, and necrosis that are hard to tell apart. Misidentification leads to the wrong treatment, wasted pesticides, higher production costs, and, in the worst case, loss of the crop.

	TomatoCare was created to fill this gap. It is a native Android diagnostic tool that runs completely offline, allowing any grower in the UAE with a smartphone to photograph a tomato leaf and receive an instant diagnosis with treatment guidance tailored to their growing method. Crucially, building it required more than adapting an existing classifier: the Capstone 1 prototype — a single classifier with an added reject class — was found to mislabel out-of-scope inputs (other crops, hands, everyday objects) as tomato diseases with high confidence, a safety defect rather than a mere accuracy shortfall. Correcting that failure was the central design driver of the project.

	The literature review in Chapter 2 identified four research and product gaps that together define the problem space. First, three of the four reviewed platforms (Farmonaut, Plantix, Agrio) require a constant internet connection and are therefore virtually unusable in the low-connectivity environments typical of UAE smallholder cultivation. Second, none of the reviewed applications offers an Arabic-language interface, a serious accessibility barrier for a large part of the UAE agricultural workforce. Third, none provides treatment recommendations adapted to UAE cultivation methods such as greenhouse, open-field, hydroponic, or saline-soil cultivation. Fourth, none reports its real-world field accuracy separately from its laboratory benchmark, leaving the accuracy a grower can actually expect in the field unmeasured.

	TomatoCare addresses all four gaps. Its AI subsystem is a three-stage cascade — a leaf gate and a tomato gate (MobileNetV3-Small) that reject out-of-scope inputs, followed by an eleven-class disease classifier (MobileNetV3-Large) covering ten tomato diseases and a healthy class. The classifier is trained on a PlantVillage-derived tomato dataset using a two-phase transfer-learning strategy and evaluated on a held-out laboratory test set, comfortably exceeding the 90% accuracy target. All three models are exported to TensorFlow Lite with float16 quantisation, for a combined footprint of 9.87 MB — well under the 15 MB budget — without loss of diagnostic accuracy.

	Each diagnosis in the application shows the condition name in English and Arabic, a calibrated confidence score, and a severity indicator (Low, Medium, High, or Critical). When confidence falls below the 60% threshold the system withholds the result and shows a Low Confidence Warning instead, so that users are not misled by an uncertain prediction. Treatment recommendations are drawn from an embedded bilingual knowledge base and filtered by the growing method selected by the user, keeping the advice relevant to their cultivation situation.

	The software development lifecycle of Agile was used to develop the project, being organized into six sprints that will run throughout Capstone 1 and planned Capstone 2. The reason behind the choice of the Agile approach is that it is more suitable to the experimental nature of machine learning model development and the need to integrate and test across streams of machine learning and Android development. The project planning, literature review, requirements specification, system architecture and design were covered in capstone 1 which this report documents. All the artefacts of the design were created at this stage, such as use case, sequence, activity, class, state chart, entity-relationship diagram, the JSON schema tree and the layered architecture diagram. These artefacts give the full technical and conceptual background to the implementation step which is planned to be implemented in Capstone 2.

	It should be noted that TomatoCare as it will be implemented has its restrictions. The application is only scoped to tomato (Solanum lycopersicum) leaf and not generalised to other crop species. It does not come with cloud synchronisation, user accounts, integration of IoT sensors, and integration of iOS. The outputs of treatment are under the form of advisory recommendations and are not legally binding prescription of agrochemicals. These limits are premeditated and suitable to a Capstone 1 and Capstone 2 deliverable; some of them, such as multi-crop support and IoT integration, are defined as the future development directions.

	On successful completion of Capstone 2, TomatoCare will provide a release-ready, bilingual Android application that will be able to perform full offline tomato leaf diagnostics on low-end devices, supported by the trained and quantised three-stage cascade and an open-source GitHub repository. By doing so, the project will directly contribute to the goals of the UAE National Food Security Strategy 2051 by putting a specialised, accessible and accurate diagnostic tool into the hands of growers, who were historically underserved by precision agriculture technologies. 
	Beyond these baseline outcomes, the AI subsystem developed in Capstone 2 contributes a safety-correct three-stage cascade (leaf gate → tomato gate → disease classifier) that hard-rejects out-of-domain inputs before any diagnosis is produced — eliminating the confident-wrong-answer failure mode observed in the Capstone 1 prototype. Measured rejection performance on the held-out evaluation set is 99.55% non-leaf rejection and 0.05% unseen-species leak rate, and confidence calibration via temperature scaling (T = 0.5889) makes the 60% low-confidence threshold statistically meaningful rather than cosmetic.
	On the eleven-class held-out laboratory test set (n = 6,683), the deployed cascade achieves 97.59% disease accuracy and 97.19% end-to-end accuracy in 9.87 MB combined model size — well under the 15 MB budget — with no INTERNET permission declared. The central empirical finding of the project, however, is the honest quantification of the laboratory-to-field gap: on real PlantDoc field photographs (n = 79) the deployed cascade reaches 77.2% end-to-end accuracy, a ≈20-point drop. Four controlled experiments — heavy environmental augmentation, MobileSAM leaf segmentation, DCGAN synthetic samples for the weakest class, and test-time normalisation to a white background — were each designed to close this gap. All four failed. A decisive composited-background experiment then isolated the cause to field-leaf appearance rather than background clutter: a perfect laboratory leaf survives a bad background (65.5%), but a field leaf on a clean background still fails (46.8%).
This experimental record is itself a contribution: it provides clear empirical evidence — not merely theoretical argument — that data-centric collection of real UAE field imagery, rather than further synthetic, augmentation, or normalisation approaches, is the correct next step. The in-app feedback flywheel implemented in this project labels and stores every field photograph the user confirms or corrects, providing the deliberate mechanism by which future versions of the model will close the remaining gap. The project therefore delivers not only a deployable bilingual offline diagnostic system, but an honestly evaluated one with a measured, mechanistic understanding of its own limitations and a concrete plan for improving them.
9	CHAPTER 9: FUTURE WORK
Several extensions follow directly from the findings in Chapter 7 and would meaningfully improve the deployed system or its supporting analysis. The list below is ordered by expected impact on the laboratory-to-field gap, which is the project's largest open issue.
1.	Real-world field-data collection via the feedback flywheel. The four failed experiments confirm that real field data is the only demonstrated way to close the laboratory-to-field gap. The in-app flywheel (§6.4) is the lowest-friction path to accumulating labelled UAE field images; even 200–400 real images per class would likely produce a measurable improvement, concentrated on the background-dependent classes (early_blight, bacterial_spot, target_spot).
2.	Motion-blur augmentation. Lighting-only augmentation was tested and did not help (§7.5); motion-blur-only augmentation — the remaining part of the supervisor's lightweight-augmentation suggestion — has not yet been isolated and is a reasonable next experiment, since hand-held capture blur is common in real use.
3.	Leaf segmentation on field data. MobileSAM segmentation showed no benefit on laboratory images because their backgrounds are already near-uniform (§7.5). Once real field images are available via the flywheel, applying background suppression before retraining may be meaningfully positive, because field backgrounds (soil, canopy, fencing) are genuinely confounding.
4.	A dedicated held-out calibration set. Re-fitting the temperature on a held-out calibration set, separate from the early-stopping data, would substantiate a tighter Expected Calibration Error than the current 0.061 and allow the confidence-calibration objective to be fully met.
5.	Per-class confidence thresholds. The single 0.60 threshold could be replaced by per-class thresholds, reducing false low-confidence warnings on strong classes (powdery_mildew 1.000, yellow_leaf_curl_virus 0.992) and raising sensitivity on weak ones (early_blight 0.943).
6.	Re-verification of gate safety metrics. The non-leaf and other-leaf rejection figures should be regenerated against a fresh held-out hard-negative set, alongside the deployed-model evaluation, to bring every reported safety number under one authoritative artefact.
7.	CameraX live capture and multi-crop generalisation. Live camera capture with real-time blur and framing feedback would help users take a sharp, centred leaf photograph, directly addressing the single-leaf assumption. Beyond Capstone 2, the cascade architecture is generalisable to other crops by training crop-specific Stage 2 gates and Stage 3 classifiers — the safety-correct pattern itself transfers.

APPENDIX A: EXPERIMENT CONFIGURATIONS (SUPPLEMENTARY)
DCGAN (Experiment 3). Latent dimension 128; image resolution 96×96; batch size 64; 150 epochs; Adam optimiser (learning rate 2×10⁻⁴, β₁ = 0.5); binary cross-entropy with logits; one-sided real-label smoothing 0.9; trained on the 2,503 bacterial_spot training images; 600 synthetic images generated and added to the training pool. Training was stable (discriminator loss ≈ 0.96, generator loss ≈ 1.52 at epoch 150; no mode collapse).
Heavy augmentation (Experiment 1). Per-image random horizontal flip; brightness jitter ±0.30; contrast factor sampled from [0.55, 1.60]; gamma γ sampled from [0.6, 1.6] with probability 0.6; hue shift ±0.06; saturation factor from [0.5, 1.6]; motion blur kernel 9×9 with probability 0.4; JPEG quality sampled from [30, 75] with probability 0.4. Applied in the tf.data pipeline (unbatch → augment → rebatch), not baked into the model graph.
Composited-background validation (§7.5.1). Laboratory leaves had their white background removed by threshold (R, G, B > 220) with 3-pixel erosion of the resulting mask, then were scaled to 78% of the canvas with ±8% position jitter and pasted onto twelve field-like background images. The resulting 165 composited images (15 per class × 11 classes) were passed through the full deployed cascade for end-to-end evaluation.
